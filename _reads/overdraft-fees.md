sections:
  - heading: One interest rate
    text: |-
      Since April 2020, banks have to charge a single yearly interest rate on overdrafts. Many charge somewhere around 35% to 40% a year.

      The rules also mean:

      - No fixed daily or monthly overdraft fees on top
      - Arranged and unarranged overdrafts cost the same
      - The rate is shown as an APR, so you can compare it with other borrowing
  - heading: What that looks like in pounds
    text: |-
      At 39.9% a year, here is roughly what borrowing costs:

      - £500 overdrawn for a month: about £14
      - £500 overdrawn for a whole year: about £200
      - £1,000 overdrawn for a month: about £28

      It adds up quietly because it is taken as interest each month, not as one fee you notice. Being £500 overdrawn all year costs about £3.85 a week.
  - heading: Buffers
    text: |-
      Some banks don't charge interest on the first part of an overdraft, such as the first £50 or £100.

      With a £50 buffer, being £500 overdrawn for a month at 39.9% means interest on £450, about £12.60 instead of £14. Your bank's app or website shows whether yours has one.
  - heading: Refused payments
    text: |-
      If there is not enough money and your bank turns down a payment, it may still charge, though many banks no longer do. Your account's tariff lists any charges. The company you were paying may also charge if a bill bounces.
  - heading: Your credit file
    text: |-
      An arranged overdraft shows on your credit file like other borrowing.

      - Staying inside your limit is normal.
      - Going past it, or being in it all the time, is what lenders tend to notice.
      - A limit you never use still counts as available credit.
  - heading: If you are in it every month
    text: |-
      That usually means the overdraft has become part of your monthly money rather than a safety net. A few things tend to help:

      - Find out how much it costs you each month, in pounds.
      - Ask your bank to lower the limit a little at a time, so the balance comes down with it.
      - If you are struggling, tell your bank. It has to treat you fairly and can offer a plan.
