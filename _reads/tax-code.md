sections:
  - heading: The number
    text: |-
      Most tax codes are a number and a letter. The number is your tax-free allowance with the last digit taken off. So **1257L** means you can earn £12,570 in the tax year, 6 April to 5 April, before income tax starts. That is the standard Personal Allowance, and it stays at £12,570 for 2026 to 2027.

      Payroll does not hand you the whole allowance at once. It spreads it across the year, so each pay gets its share.

      - Paid monthly on 1257L, about £1,047.50 of each month's pay is tax-free.
      - Paid weekly, it is about £242 a week.
  - heading: A worked example
    text: |-
      Say you are paid £2,500 in a month on 1257L, in England.

      - Tax-free share for the month: £1,047.50
      - Taxable pay: £2,500 minus £1,047.50 = £1,452.50
      - Income tax at 20%: about £290.50

      That is why two people on the same pay can see different tax. A different code means a different tax-free share.
  - heading: The letter
    text: |-
      The letter tells payroll and HMRC what kind of allowance you have.

      - **L**: the standard allowance.
      - **M**: your partner passed you £1,260 of their allowance through Marriage Allowance, so your number goes up, for example 1383M.
      - **N**: you passed £1,260 of yours to your partner, so your number goes down, for example 1131N.
      - **T**: there is something else in your code HMRC wants to review.

      A code that starts with **S** means you pay Scottish income tax rates, and **C** means Welsh rates.
  - heading: Codes with no number
    text: |-
      Some codes don't give an allowance at all. They tell payroll to tax this job in one fixed way.

      - **0T**: no tax-free allowance on this job, so tax starts from the first pound. You often see it when your allowance is used up elsewhere, or a new employer does not have your details yet.
      - **BR**: everything from this job is taxed at the basic rate, 20%. Common on a second job, because your allowance is used by the first.
      - **D0**: everything taxed at the higher rate, 40%.
      - **D1**: everything taxed at the additional rate, 45%.
      - **NT**: no tax taken at all.
  - heading: W1, M1, X or NONCUM at the end
    text: |-
      These mark an emergency code. Normally your tax is worked out across the whole year so far. On an emergency code, each pay is taxed on its own, as if it were the only pay of the year.

      Here is how that plays out. You start a new job in October on 1257L M1, and you earned nothing earlier in the tax year. By October, seven months of allowance have built up, about £7,332.50, so your first £2,000 of pay would normally be tax-free. On M1, only one month's allowance counts, so about £190 of tax comes off that pay.

      That money is not lost. Once HMRC has your details, your code changes and the overpaid tax usually comes back through your pay, or after the tax year ends.
  - heading: K at the start
    text: |-
      A K code means you have income or a benefit that is not taxed anywhere else, and it is more than your allowance. A company car, or tax owed from an earlier year, are common reasons.

      So instead of taking an allowance away from your taxable pay, a K code adds to it. **K475** adds £4,750 over the year, about £395.83 a month. Payroll can't take more than half of any one pay in tax because of a K code.
  - heading: If yours looks wrong
    text: |-
      Your code is HMRC's best guess from what it knows. It is worth a look when you start a new job, get a pay rise, start or stop a benefit like a company car, or see a big change in tax.

      - See your code, and how HMRC worked it out, in the HMRC app or your personal tax account on GOV.UK.
      - Check the employers, pensions and benefits listed there are right, and update anything that isn't.
      - If too much tax was taken, it usually comes back through your pay once the code is fixed. If not, HMRC sends a calculation after the tax year ends.
